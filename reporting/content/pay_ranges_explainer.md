## The market range

Every role in your dashboard is placed on a market range rather than against a single number, because a single number would hide how wide the market actually is.

A range has three points. The **lower quartile** is the 25th percentile: a quarter of the market pays less than this, three quarters pay more. The **median** is the 50th percentile, the midpoint of the market. The **upper quartile** is the 75th percentile: three quarters of the market pays less, a quarter pays more. You will see these written as LQ, median and UQ throughout.

Two things follow from that, and both matter more than they first appear.

**A range is not a target.** The quartiles describe where the market sits. Where you choose to sit within it is a decision about your own reward strategy, and it is worth making deliberately rather than by accident. Paying at the median is not a pass mark and paying at the upper quartile is not a prize.

**Below the lower quartile is the one figure that usually needs action.** It means you are paying less than three quarters of the comparable market for that role, which is where retention risk concentrates. If you read nothing else in your dashboard, read that count.

### What sits behind the range

Your pay ranges are drawn from **{pay_employers} technology employers** advertising comparable roles in {pay_basis}, taken from a national database of more than {salary_records} UK salary records. Benefits work differently: they come from survey and audit evidence covering **{benefit_employers} employers**, because employers rarely publish benefit detail in a job advert.

Two methods, two counts, and neither is a subset of the other. The Methodology page in your dashboard covers this in full.

## The same idea, three sets of words

Your dashboard has three surfaces, and they describe market position at three levels of detail. This is deliberate: the home page summarises, the Pay dashboard is precise, and the Benefits report adds a category of its own. It does mean the same role can be described three ways, so here they are side by side.

| Where you are | What you will see | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Home, Trends | Below market | Under the median |
| Home, Trends | At market | Median to upper quartile |
| Home, Trends | Above market | Above the upper quartile |
| Pay dashboard | Below LQ | Under the lower quartile |
| Pay dashboard | LQ to Median | Between the lower quartile and the median |
| Pay dashboard | Median to UQ | Between the median and the upper quartile |
| Pay dashboard | Above UQ | Above the upper quartile |
| Benefits report | Below market | A gap against the comparator |
| Benefits report | Mixed | Competitive in part, behind in part |
| Benefits report | At market | In line with the comparator |
| Benefits report | Above market | Ahead of the comparator |

The home page collapses four bands into three, and it splits them **at the median**. So a role shown as "below market" on the home page is anything under the median, which on the Pay dashboard appears as either "Below LQ" or "LQ to Median". The counts agree; only the labels differ.

### Why "Above UQ" is not coloured as good news

Above the upper quartile renders in blue, not green. Green is used for "Median to UQ", which is the competitive band.

That is not an oversight. Paying above the upper quartile can be exactly right, for a genuinely scarce skill or a person carrying more than the job title suggests. It can equally be a role that has drifted, a title that no longer matches the work, or a legacy arrangement nobody has revisited. It is worth a look either way, which is what blue is saying. Only red, on "Below LQ", is telling you something is likely wrong.

## Part-time roles and FTE

This is the part that surprises people, and it is worth reading even if you have no part-time roles today.

Market ranges are **full-time rates**. So when your dashboard compares a part-time person to the market, it first converts their pay to a full-time equivalent by dividing their actual salary by their FTE. Someone on 0.5 FTE earning £30,000 is compared to the market as £60,000.

Your **salary bill**, by contrast, uses what you actually pay. The same person counts as £30,000 towards the cost of any pay review you model.

Both are correct, and they answer different questions. Positioning asks "are we paying this person the market rate for the work they do". Cost asks "what does our payroll total". Using actual pay for positioning would make every part-timer look underpaid, and using full-time-equivalent pay for the bill would inflate your payroll by tens of thousands.

The practical consequence: **a part-time person paid exactly the market median for their hours will still show as at market, not below it.** If a part-time role looks wrong to you, check the FTE figure first. It is shown alongside every person in the By person view on Your Organisation.

## Other terms you will meet

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Compa-ratio | Current salary divided by the market median, as a percentage. Under 100% is below the median, over 100% is above. A quick way to compare roles with very different salaries. |
| Position | Which of the market bands a role or person falls into. It always means market position, never job title or location. |
| Comparator group | The slice of the market you are measured against, defined by function, industry, job level and location. A narrow comparator is the point: a wider one would be a bigger number and a worse benchmark. |
| Pay compression | When the gap between grades narrows, usually because rises at the bottom of the market outpace those above. It makes career paths hard to set out, particularly for supervisory roles. |
| FTE | Full-time equivalent. 1.0 is full time, 0.5 is half time. |

## The status words on Your Organisation

Five words describe where each item on Your Organisation stands, and they are not interchangeable.

| Status | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Benchmarked | We have market data for this and it is live in your dashboard. |
| Updated | You have edited it since the last refresh. The change is already flowing through your Pay dashboards. |
| Awaiting benchmark | You have added a role and sent it to us. We are matching it to the market. |
| Awaiting market data | You have added a benefit and sent it to us. We are benchmarking it. |
| Under review | You have changed an existing benefit. Benefits are qualitative, so a specialist re-checks it against the market rather than a figure being recalculated. |

Anything you add or change goes to your TwentySix consultant, and a new benchmark comes back within **{turnaround}**.

### One thing worth knowing about edits

Editing a salary on Your Organisation changes it **everywhere**, immediately: the home page verdict, the Pay dashboards, the attention list and any pay review you model. There is no separate save-and-publish step and no version history. If you want to explore a change rather than make it, use Prep a Pay Review on the Pay dashboard, which models cost without touching your data.
